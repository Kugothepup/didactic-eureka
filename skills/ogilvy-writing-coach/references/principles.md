# The Principles

The detection engine for the audit. For each rule: what it means, what a violation looks like in real text, the severity it usually lands at, and a before/after so your feedback stays concrete. Quote the draft exactly and show the fix — never just name the problem.

**Contents**
- Ogilvy's 10 rules (1–10)
- The Roman-Raphaelson principles (the substance behind Rule 1)
- Quick-reference checklist

The source is Ogilvy's memo of September 7, 1982, "How To Write," which opens: *"The better you write, the higher you will go… People who think well, write well. Good writing is not a natural gift. You have to learn to write well."*

## Ogilvy's 10 rules

### Rule 1 — Read the Roman-Raphaelson book. Read it three times.
> "Read the Roman-Raphaelson book on writing. Read it three times."

This rule has no text to flag — it points at the substance. The book is *Writing That Works* (Roman & Raphaelson, Harper & Row, 1981). Its principles are listed below and power most of the other checks. Treat "read it three times" as: these moves should be so internalized they're automatic. When you cite a Roman-Raphaelson principle in the audit, you are enforcing Rule 1.

### Rule 2 — Write the way you talk. Naturally.
> "Write the way you talk. Naturally."

**Means:** The prose should sound like a real person speaking — the author's own voice, not a corporate costume.
**Violation cues:** Phrases no one would say aloud ("Please be advised that," "It is incumbent upon us," "Per my last email"); stiff third-person distance; a tone wildly different from how the author actually talks; the read-aloud test failing.
**Severity:** Moderate usually; Critical if the whole piece is so stilted the reader can't connect.
**Before:** "Please be advised that the undersigned will be unavailable for the duration of the forthcoming week."
**After:** "I'll be out all next week."

### Rule 3 — Short words, short sentences, short paragraphs.
> "Use short words, short sentences and short paragraphs."

**Means:** Make it effortless to read. Length is friction.
**Violation cues:** Sentences running past ~25–30 words or stacking three+ clauses; long Latinate words where a short Anglo-Saxon one exists (utilize→use, commence→start, facilitate→help, in order to→to); paragraphs that are a wall of text with no break.
**Severity:** Moderate for general bloat; Critical when density is bad enough that the reader gives up.
**Before:** "We are currently in the process of endeavoring to facilitate a comprehensive optimization of our operational methodologies."
**After:** "We're working to make our operations simpler."

### Rule 4 — Never use jargon.
> "Never use jargon words like reconceptualize, demassification, attitudinally, judgmentally. They are hallmarks of a pretentious ass."

**Means:** Jargon usually hides a lack of understanding, or shows off. Write so an 8th-grader could follow.
**Violation cues:** Buzzwords and vogue terms (leverage, synergy, ideate, circle back, bandwidth, paradigm, holistic, robust, seamless, best-in-class, move the needle, low-hanging fruit); needless acronyms; nominalizations (turning verbs into nouns: "make a decision"→"decide," "provide assistance"→"help").
**Severity:** Moderate normally; Critical when the jargon is so thick the meaning is lost. Note the exception: a precise technical term that *this specific audience* uses daily is not jargon — don't flag it.
**Before:** "Let's leverage our core competencies to ideate a holistic, best-in-class solution."
**After:** "Let's use what we're good at to design the best solution we can."

### Rule 5 — Never write more than two pages on any subject.
> "Never write more than two pages on any subject."

**Means:** If it can't fit in two pages, it isn't clear enough yet. Most books should be posts; most posts should be paragraphs.
**Violation cues:** Length out of proportion to the point; repetition; three examples where one would do; preamble that delays the substance.
**Severity:** Moderate when padded; Critical when sheer length means the intended reader will never finish.
**Fix pattern:** Name what could be cut and roughly how much shorter it should be — "this is ~3 pages of content carrying one page of point; cut the background section and the second case study."

### Rule 6 — Check your quotations.
> "Check your quotations."

**Means:** Verify anything you present as fact — quotes, names, numbers, claims.
**Violation cues:** Quotations, statistics, dates, attributions, or confident factual claims with no source — anything that would mislead or embarrass if it's wrong.
**Severity:** Minor to flag for verification; Critical if a load-bearing claim is shaky and the stakes are high. You usually can't verify external facts yourself — flag them and tell the writer to check, rather than asserting they're right or wrong.
**Fix pattern:** "You state '73% of buyers prefer X' — confirm the source and date before sending; an unbacked stat invites pushback."

### Rule 7 — Don't send it the day you write it. Read it aloud the next morning, then edit.
> "Never send a letter or a memo on the day you write it. Read it aloud the next morning—and then edit it."

**Means:** Distance reveals flaws; the ear catches what the eye skims.
**In the audit:** You can't make the writer wait a day, but you *can* perform the read-aloud pass for them now (see SKILL.md workflow step 2) and flag every place the spoken version trips. For genuinely high-stakes pieces, add the reminder to sleep on it and re-read aloud tomorrow.
**Severity:** The read-aloud findings get scored on their own merits (usually Moderate). The "sleep on it" reminder is advice, not a flagged violation.

### Rule 8 — If it's important, get a colleague to improve it.
> "If it is something important, get a colleague to improve it."

**Means:** A second set of eyes catches what the author is blind to.
**In the audit:** You *are* that second reader — that's the whole service. For high-stakes pieces (a board memo, a launch announcement, a contract email), remind the writer that a trusted human colleague's read still adds value on top of yours, especially for tone and political nuance you can't see.
**Severity:** Advice, not a flagged violation.

### Rule 9 — Make it crystal clear what you want the recipient to do.
> "Before you send your letter or memo, make sure it is crystal clear what you want the recipient to do."

**Means:** The reader should finish knowing the exact next step — and it should be easy to find.
**Violation cues:** No call to action; a buried or vague ask ("let me know your thoughts"); multiple competing asks with no priority; the action hidden in paragraph four.
**Severity:** Usually Critical — if the reader doesn't know what to do, the writing failed, however polished.
**Before:** "…so there are a few things we could potentially explore here and I'd value your perspective when you get a chance."
**After:** "Can you approve the budget by Thursday? That's the one thing I need from you."

### Rule 10 — If you want ACTION, don't write. Go tell the guy.
> "If you want ACTION, don't write. Go and tell the guy what you want."

**Means:** Writing is the wrong tool for some jobs. Anything contentious, sensitive, urgent, or requiring back-and-forth usually moves faster face-to-face or by call.
**In the audit:** Check this *first*. When it applies, flag it at the very top — then audit the writing anyway, since they may still need to send something.
**Severity:** Critical when the medium itself is the core problem (e.g. a long email trying to resolve a conflict that needs a conversation).
**Fix pattern:** "This is asking three people to reverse a decision — that's a conversation, not an email. A 10-minute call gets you there faster. If you must put it in writing, lead with the ask and keep it to five lines."

## The Roman-Raphaelson principles

The substance behind Rule 1, from *Writing That Works*. These reinforce and sharpen Ogilvy's rules; cite them by name in the audit.

- **Lead with the point.** Busy readers decide in seconds whether to keep going. Put the most important thing first; never bury it. *Cue:* the real message appears halfway down.
- **Cut throat-clearing openings.** Delete warm-up phrases that delay the substance ("I am writing to inform you that…," "I just wanted to reach out…"). *Cue:* the first sentence carries no information.
- **Make every word earn its place.** If a word can go without loss, cut it. *Cue:* "in order to," "the fact that," "at this point in time," "actually," "really," "very."
- **Prefer the active voice and strong verbs.** Active is shorter, clearer, and assigns responsibility. *Cue:* "a decision was made," "it has been determined," "mistakes were made." *Fix:* say who did what — "we decided."
- **Don't turn verbs into nouns (nominalization).** *Cue:* "conduct an investigation"→"investigate," "reach a conclusion"→"conclude," "give consideration to"→"consider."
- **One idea per paragraph.** Each paragraph should carry a single thought; break the wall.
- **Be specific and concrete.** Abstractions slide off the reader; details stick. *Cue:* "significant improvements"→"cut load time from 8s to 2s."
- **Don't hedge.** Qualifiers drain confidence and clutter the line. *Cue:* "I think we should maybe perhaps consider possibly…" Say it once, plainly.
- **Don't overstate.** Exaggeration ("revolutionary," "game-changing," "world-class") makes readers distrust you. Let the facts carry the weight.
- **Use structure to aid scanning.** Where a list or a heading makes the content easier to take in, use it — but don't manufacture structure where prose reads fine.
- **Read it aloud.** If you stumble saying it, the reader stumbles reading it. (This is also Ogilvy's Rule 7.)

## Quick-reference checklist

Run this pass over any draft:

1. **Medium** — should this even be written, or is it a conversation? (R10)
2. **The ask** — is the next step unmistakable and easy to find? (R9)
3. **The point** — is it first, or buried? (R-R)
4. **Length** — under two pages, no padding? (R5)
5. **Sentences & words** — short, plain, Anglo-Saxon over Latinate? (R3)
6. **Jargon** — any buzzwords, vogue terms, or needless acronyms? (R4)
7. **Voice** — does it sound like a person talking? (R2)
8. **Active voice** — verbs doing the work, no hidden actors? (R-R)
9. **Read-aloud** — where does the spoken version trip? (R7)
10. **Facts** — any quote, stat, or claim that needs checking? (R6)
